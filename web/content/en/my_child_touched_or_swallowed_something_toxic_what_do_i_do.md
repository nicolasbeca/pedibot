## What it is
Poisoning happens after contact with a substance that can harm a child's health. Contact can happen by swallowing, breathing in, or touching the skin [2]. A poison is any substance that is dangerous to the body. It can be swallowed, inhaled, injected, or absorbed through the skin [6]. Examples include medicines in too-high doses, illegal drug overdoses, carbon monoxide from gas burners, household products like laundry powder or furniture polish, pesticides, indoor and outdoor plants, and metals like lead and mercury [6]. Effects can range from short-term illness to brain injury, coma, and death [6].

## What you can do at home
If the contact was by swallowing [2]:
- Separate the child from the toxic substance.
- If there is something solid in the mouth, visible and reachable, remove it. Never do this blindly, as it could make things worse.
- Do not try to make the child vomit [2][3].
- Do not give water, milk, oil, or any other food [2].
- Try to determine how much was swallowed.
- Call your national poison information service, or your emergency number, if the child is not responding normally, is not breathing well, or has a bad color [2].

If the contact was on the skin or eyes [2]:
- Remove all contaminated clothing.
- Wash with plenty of warm running water (shower) for 15–20 minutes.

If the contact was by breathing in gas or smoke [2]:
- Open the windows.
- Get outside into fresh air as quickly as possible.
- Call the emergency phone number.

The Poison Information phone number is available 24 hours a day, every day: your national poison information service [4]. In the United States, call the local poison control center at your national poison information service immediately if you suspect poisoning [6].

Do not do these things [3]:
- Do not treat a non-toxic ingestion as if it were harmless and ignore it.
- Do not underestimate the toxicity.
- Do not induce vomiting.
- Do not perform gastric lavage (stomach pumping). It is only possibly indicated for a recent ingestion (less than 1 hour) of a potentially lethal large amount of a toxic substance, in a patient who is conscious or intubated.
- Do not give activated charcoal when it is not indicated.
- Do not give water, milk, or activated charcoal after swallowing household or industrial products. In exceptional cases, careful evaluation may change this recommendation.
- Do not give flumazenil to patients with suspected tricyclic antidepressant ingestion or who have had seizures from poisoning.
- Do not give N-acetylcysteine after acute paracetamol ingestion without checking toxic blood levels, when results can be obtained first.

One exception: massive paracetamol ingestion (more than 30 g) requires immediate start of the antidote [1].

## When to see a doctor or go to the emergency department
Go to the emergency department when [5]:
- The Poison Information Institute or Emergency Services tells you to.
- Medicines, drugs of abuse, cleaning products, garden products, or car products are involved.
- The child has symptoms.
- You have doubts that were not resolved by phone.

If they recommend going to an emergency department, go as soon as possible. If possible, bring the container of the product involved [5].

## Common questions
How can I prevent poisoning at home?
- Store medicines and toxic products out of reach and sight, in high places, locked, or with safety closures. Do this immediately after use [4].
- Keep them in their original containers with original labels. Avoid containers that could be confused with drinks [4].
- Use products with safety caps and close them well [4].
- Check instructions and dose before giving any medicine [4].
- Keep few medicines at home and do not take them in front of children [4].
- Keep bags, purses, or clothing that may contain medicines out of children's reach, including guests' items [4].
- Never tell children that medicine is candy [4].
- Do not mix different household products [4].
- If you have children under 6 years old, use liquid or powder detergents, not capsules [4].
- Check gas, boilers, and any combustion system regularly [4].

Should I make my child vomit?
No. Do not try to induce vomiting [2][3].
