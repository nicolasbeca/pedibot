## What it is

Growth patterns, or growth standards, are tools that let health workers judge how a child is growing [1]. They are not just a description of how children grow in one time and place; they are built with norms or goals in them, so they can be used to make judgements about a child's growth [2].

The World Health Organization (WHO) standards were built from a joint sample of children from six participating countries, which makes them truly international, unlike the earlier international reference pattern that came from children in a single country [2]. They also make breastfeeding the biological norm and treat the breastfed infant as the normative model of growth [2]. The earlier reference pattern was based on the growth of children who were fed by non-natural means [2].

The WHO standards include new growth indicators beyond height and weight, such as skinfold thickness, which are particularly useful for tracking the growing epidemic of childhood obesity [2]. The study was also longitudinal, which allows patterns of growth velocity to be built [2].

Because of these standards, health care providers can diagnose undernutrition and overweight before children cross a growth threshold, since the patterns allow early detection of children who are undernourished or overnourished [1][2].

## What you can do at home

- Keep your child's growth checks and appointments, so that changes over time can be seen [1].
- Support, protect and promote breastfeeding [1].
- Remember that the standards treat the breastfed infant as the model of normal growth [2].
- Ask your health care provider to explain your child's measurements on the chart, rather than comparing your child with other children yourself [1].

## When to see a doctor or go to the emergency department

The sources do not list specific emergency warning signs for growth percentiles. What they describe is that the patterns allow health care providers to detect, early, children who are undernourished or overnourished, and to diagnose undernutrition and overweight before a child crosses a growth threshold [1][2].

Because of this, see your doctor if you are worried about your child's weight, height or growth over time, or if a growth check shows a change that concerns you [1][2]. If your child seems very unwell, contact your local emergency number or go to the emergency department.

## Common questions

**Do the new standards change how many children are considered undernourished or overweight?**

Yes. The estimates changed because the growth pattern of the new standards differs from the old reference criteria, especially during infancy [1]. The size of the change varied with age, sex, the growth indicator used, and the underlying nutritional state of the population assessed [1].

**What differences were seen?**

Stunting — height that is too low for age — was greater during infancy when the new WHO patterns were used than with the earlier international reference pattern [1]. There was a substantial increase in rates of underweight during the first half of infancy, from 0 to 6 months, before that rate began to fall [1]. For wasting — low weight for height — the main difference between the new and old patterns appears during infancy, up to a height of about 70 centimetres, when wasting rates are substantially higher with the new WHO patterns [1]. For overweight, using the new WHO patterns gives a higher prevalence, which varies with age, sex and the nutritional state of the population studied [1].

**Why does it matter that the standards are prescriptive?**

A prescriptive approach does not just update information on how children grow in presumably healthy populations at a given time and place; it explicitly recognises the need for patterns that are instruments for making value judgements [2]. It has been suggested that the current obesity epidemic affecting many developed countries could have been detected earlier if a prescriptive international pattern had existed more than 30 years ago [2].

**Does growth look the same everywhere?**

The joint sample from six countries emphasises that, if children's health and care needs are met, children's growth is similar across the major regions of the world [2].
