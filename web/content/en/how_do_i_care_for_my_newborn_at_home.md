## What it is

Caring for a newborn in the first 0 to 12 weeks involves meeting their basic needs for sleep, food, cleanliness, and comfort [1]. Newborns need frequent feedings and nappy changes, and they can have health issues that are different from older children and adults, like nappy rash and cradle cap [2]. It is normal to feel uneasy at first; you can ask your health care provider for help if you need it [2].

## What you can do at home

- **Helping your baby to sleep:** Always place your baby on their back to sleep to reduce the risk of sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS) [1]. Follow safe sleep practices as advised by your health care provider [1].
- **Changing nappies:** Change your baby's nappy frequently to keep their skin clean and dry [1]. Clean the area gently with water or fragrance-free wipes, and pat dry before putting on a fresh nappy [1].
- **Nappy rash:** To prevent or treat nappy rash, change nappies often and let the skin air dry when possible [1]. Apply a barrier cream (like zinc oxide) if the skin looks red or sore [1]. Avoid using talcum powder or harsh soaps [1].
- **Soothing a crying baby:** Try feeding, burping, changing, or holding your baby skin-to-skin to calm them [1]. Swaddling or gentle rocking can also help [1]. If crying continues, check for signs of illness or discomfort [1].
- **Washing and bathing:** You do not need to bathe your baby every day; 2 to 3 times a week is enough [1]. Use warm (not hot) water and a mild, fragrance-free baby cleanser [1]. Clean the face, neck, hands, and nappy area daily with a soft cloth [1]. Never leave your baby alone in the bath [1].
- **What you'll need:** Have a safe cot with a firm mattress, a fitted sheet, nappies, baby clothes, blankets (if used, placed securely), and baby toiletries ready before you bring your baby home [1].

## When to see a doctor or go to the emergency department

Seek medical help if your baby shows any of these warning signs [2]:
- Has a fever (check with a thermometer; ask your provider for the right temperature for a newborn) [2].
- Is not feeding well or is refusing feeds [2].
- Has fewer wet or dirty nappies than usual [2].
- Is unusually sleepy, hard to wake, or floppy [2].
- Has a persistent cry or is inconsolable [2].
- Has a bulging soft spot on the head, or a stiff neck [2].
- Has a blue or pale colour to the skin, lips, or tongue [2].
- Has trouble breathing or is breathing fast [2].
- Has a seizure or convulsion [2].
- Has a rash that does not fade when pressed (check with a glass) [2].
- Has a nappy rash that is severe, blistered, or spreading [1].

If you are ever worried, call your health care provider or go to the emergency department right away [2]. Trust your instincts—you know your baby best [2].

## Common questions

**How often should I feed my newborn?**
Newborns need frequent feedings, usually every 2 to 3 hours, whether breastfed or formula-fed [2]. Feed on demand and watch for hunger cues like rooting or sucking on hands [2]. If you are unsure about feeding amounts or schedules, ask your health care provider [2].

**Is it normal for my baby to have cradle cap?**
Yes, cradle cap is a common condition in newborns [2]. It looks like scaly, greasy patches on the scalp [2]. You can wash your baby's hair with a mild baby shampoo and gently brush the scales away [1]. It usually clears on its own, but talk to your provider if it spreads or looks infected [2].

**When can I give my baby a real bath?**
You can start sponge baths right away, but wait until the umbilical cord stump falls off (usually 1 to 2 weeks) before submerging your baby in a tub [1]. Until then, clean your baby with a soft, damp cloth [1]. After the stump heals, you can use a small baby tub with warm water [1].
