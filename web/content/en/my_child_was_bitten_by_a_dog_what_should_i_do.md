## What it is

Rabies is a viral disease that attacks the brain and spinal cord [1][3]. Dogs cause up to 99% of human rabies cases, and the virus spreads through saliva, usually by bites, scratches or saliva touching the eyes, mouth or an open wound [1][3]. Children between 5 and 14 years old are frequent victims [3]. Once symptoms appear, rabies is fatal in almost 100% of cases, but deaths are preventable if care is started promptly after the bite [1][3].

## What you can do at home

- Wash the wound thoroughly with water and soap for at least 15 minutes, as soon as possible after the bite or scratch [6].
- Then go to a health facility the same day. Washing is only the first step; the person also needs a course of rabies vaccine, and sometimes rabies immunoglobulin [1][6].
- Do not wait to see whether the wound gets worse. Care should be sought immediately and always after a bite or scratch from a possibly rabid animal [1].
- If the bite is bleeding, or if saliva touched the eyes, mouth or broken skin, tell the doctor — this counts as a severe exposure and needs immunoglobulin as well as vaccine [2].
- The rabies vaccine is given as a course, and the schedule is the one the doctor or the leaflet gives [6].
- If the biting dog is known, ask the doctor whether the animal should be observed or tested [5].
- Keep children away from unknown dogs, and teach them not to approach animals while they eat or sleep [5].

## When to see a doctor or go to the emergency department

Go to a doctor or the emergency department straight after any bite or scratch from a dog or other mammal, even if it looks minor [1][2]. This is urgent in these situations:

- Any bite or scratch that breaks the skin, with or without bleeding [2].
- Saliva from an animal touching the eyes, mouth or broken skin [2].
- Direct contact with a bat [2].
- Tingling, pricking or burning at the wound site, fever or pain — these can be early symptoms, and by then the disease is almost always fatal [7].

## Common questions

**Does every dog bite need the vaccine?**
Not every bite carries the same risk, and the decision depends on the type of contact. Minor scratches or nibbling of uncovered skin without bleeding need wound washing and immediate vaccination [2]. Bites that go through the skin, or saliva on mucous membranes, need washing, vaccination and rabies immunoglobulin [2]. A doctor assesses the risk in each case [5].

**How long do we have?**
There is no safe delay. Care should be sought immediately after the exposure [1]. The time between the bite and symptoms is usually 2 to 3 months, but can be as short as one week, so starting care early matters [7].

**Can rabies still be prevented after symptoms start?**
No. Once symptoms appear, rabies is virtually 100% fatal, and care then focuses on comfort [3][5]. That is why the vaccine course is started before any symptoms appear [1].

**Are bites from other animals a risk?**
Rabies affects mammals, including cats, livestock and wildlife [3]. In the Americas, bats are now the main source of human rabies [4]. Bites from rodents are not known to transmit rabies [4].

**Can a person pass rabies to another person?**
Transmission between people through bites or saliva is theoretically possible but has never been confirmed [4].

**What about preventing rabies before a bite?**
Vaccination before exposure is recommended for people in high-risk jobs, such as laboratory workers handling the virus or wildlife rangers, and sometimes for travel to areas where rabies is common [6]. It does not replace the need for care after a bite [6].
