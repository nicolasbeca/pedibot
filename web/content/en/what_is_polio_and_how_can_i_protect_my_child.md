## What it is

Polio is caused by polioviruses, which are small RNA viruses belonging to the enterovirus genus [6]. There are three types of poliovirus: type 1, type 2 and type 3 [6]. The virus is stable at low pH and resists many enzymes, which allows it to pass through the stomach and gut [6]. The only reservoir for polioviruses is humans [2].

Polio was once widespread worldwide, and because contact with the virus usually happened in childhood, it became known as "infantile paralysis" [3]. According to the WHO, vaccination campaigns have protected more than 20 million people from paralysis and at least 1.5 million people from death from polio [3]. Worldwide cases have fallen by 99.9% compared with the 1980s [3]. Five of six WHO regions are certified polio-free: the Americas (1994), the Western Pacific (2000), Europe (2002), South-East Asia (2014) and Africa (2020) [3]. The WHO Eastern Mediterranean Region is not yet certified polio-free [3]. Wild poliovirus type 2 was declared eradicated in 2015 and type 3 in 2019, so only wild poliovirus type 1 still circulates, in Afghanistan and Pakistan [3]. Outbreaks with circulating vaccine-derived polioviruses, mainly type 2, currently occur especially in Africa [3]. The WHO declared a Public Health Emergency of International Concern in 2014, and this has been extended repeatedly [3].

A person is contagious as long as the viruses are excreted [7]. Polioviruses can be found in throat secretions from 36 hours after infection and may persist there for up to a week [7]. Excretion in stool begins after 2 to 3 days and can last up to 6 weeks [7]. In very rare cases, such as in people with immunodeficiency, excretion can last months or years [7].

Polioviruses can be detected in wastewater samples even when no confirmed clinical cases are known [4]. Such a finding means that one or more people are infected and excrete the virus in their stool, but these people do not have to be clinically ill [4]. The viral load in wastewater is usually very low, so there is no danger for people who work with wastewater, and no booster vaccinations beyond those already recommended are needed for them [4].

## What you can do at home

- Make sure your child's polio vaccination is up to date according to your country's current recommendations [2].
- Check the vaccination status before travelling and refresh it if needed [2].
- Some countries require proof of a booster dose of inactivated polio vaccine when leaving the country [2].
- There is no specific antiviral treatment for polio; treatment is symptomatic [10].
- After the acute phase, longer physiotherapy and orthopaedic follow-up are usually needed, depending on the course of the illness [10].

## When to see a doctor or go to the emergency department

- Sudden weakness or paralysis (acute flaccid paralysis) needs medical assessment [1].
- In Guillain-Barré syndrome, which is the most common cause of acute flaccid paralysis today, paralysis is usually symmetrical and may develop over up to 10 days, and fever, headache, nausea and vomiting are often absent [1].
- If meningitis or encephalitis is suspected, non-paralytic forms of polio and other causes should be considered [1].
- If polio is clinically suspected, diagnosis must be confirmed by sending two stool samples to a national reference centre for poliomyelitis and enteroviruses, announced by phone or email [10].

## Common questions

**How is polio diagnosed?**
Stool samples are best for detecting polioviruses [10]. The virus can be found in stool in about 80% of cases during the first 14 days of illness [10]. Throat swabs and cerebrospinal fluid may also be used [10]. Virus culture increases diagnostic sensitivity, and molecular methods such as PCR and sequencing distinguish wild-type, vaccine and vaccine-derived strains [10].

**Is there a treatment?**
No specific antiviral treatment is available; care is symptomatic [10].

**Why do wastewater findings matter?**
Wastewater testing can detect poliovirus introductions early [2]. When vaccine-derived polioviruses are detected at one place for at least 2 months, or at different places at the same time without overlapping catchment areas, the WHO classifies them as circulating vaccine-derived polioviruses [4]. People who are not sufficiently vaccinated against polio may then be at risk [4].

**What about vaccination?**
In Germany, the Standing Committee on Vaccination stopped recommending oral polio vaccine in 1998 and recommended inactivated polio vaccine instead, because oral vaccine caused one to two vaccine-associated paralytic polio cases per year [2]. Age-appropriate polio protection according to current recommendations is advised [2].
