## What it is
Bronchiolitis is an infection that affects the small airways (bronchioles) of the lungs [6]. It is caused by viruses, most commonly the Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV), although other respiratory viruses can also cause it [6]. It occurs in epidemics from October to March and mostly affects children under 2 years of age [6].

It usually starts like a normal cold, with cough and nasal mucus. There may be fever [3]. After 2-4 days, difficulty breathing may appear. You may notice your child breathing faster, being irritable, and having trouble feeding [3]. Sometimes you can hear noises in the chest, see the ribs showing, the chest sinking in, or the belly moving with each breath [3].

Most bronchiolitis cases are mild and can be managed with general measures and follow-up with your primary care pediatrician [1]. However, some cases may have more severe breathing difficulty and require oxygen in the hospital [1]. Babies under 3 months, those with previous health problems, and premature babies are especially vulnerable [1].

Currently, there is no medication that cures bronchiolitis [1]. In most cases, it improves within 2 weeks, although the cough may last longer [1].

## What you can do at home
- Do nasal washes to keep the nose clear of mucus [7].
- Offer small, frequent feeds to maintain good nutrition and hydration [7].
- Keep your child in a semi-upright position to make breathing easier [7].
- Give fever medication if there is fever [7].
- Strictly avoid exposure to tobacco smoke [7].
- Wash hands frequently among family members to prevent spreading the virus [7].

Things that are NOT useful: antibiotics (they do not work against viruses), cough syrups, mucolytics, corticosteroids, or respiratory physiotherapy [5]. Inhaled medications like salbutamol have not been shown to improve the course of the disease [2].

Prevention includes hand washing, avoiding contact between babies and people with colds, and avoiding crowded enclosed spaces during bronchiolitis season, such as shopping malls [5]. Breastfeeding helps protect against these infections [5]. There is also an immunizing antibody (Nirsevimab) against RSV that offers immediate protection, preventing hospitalization in more than 80% of cases [5]. It is safe and may have mild side effects: skin rash, fever, and pain at the injection site [5].

It is recommended not to take your child to daycare if they have been diagnosed with acute bronchiolitis, as it spreads from person to person through coughing, sneezing, and contact with contaminated surfaces [1].

## When to see a doctor or go to the emergency department
Go to the emergency department if your child:

- Is breathing worse or faster each time [4].
- Stops breathing for a few seconds or their lips change color (blue-purple) [4].
- Has difficulty feeding, refuses feeds, or vomits [4].
- Is not wetting diapers and has a dry mouth and tongue (signs of dehydration) [4].
- Seems very lethargic or whiny [4].
- Has very high fever [4].

## Common questions
**How long does bronchiolitis last?**
Most cases improve within 2 weeks, although the cough can last longer [1].

**Can my child go to daycare?**
No. It is recommended not to take a child with acute bronchiolitis to daycare because it spreads from person to person [1].

**What symptoms should I watch for at home?**
Watch your child's breathing, difficulty feeding, skin color and general condition, and fever [1].

**Is there a vaccine or prevention?**
Yes. There is an immunizing antibody (Nirsevimab) against RSV that prevents hospitalization in more than 80% of cases [5]. Hand washing and avoiding contact with sick people also help [5].
