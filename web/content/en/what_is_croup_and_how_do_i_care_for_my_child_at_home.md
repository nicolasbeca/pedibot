## What it is

Croup is an inflammation of the vocal cords (larynx) and windpipe (trachea) [2]. It causes difficulty breathing, a barking cough, and a hoarse voice [2]. The cause is usually a virus, often parainfluenza virus; other causes include allergies and reflux [2].

Croup often starts out like a cold, and then the vocal cords and windpipe become swollen, causing the hoarseness and the cough [2]. There may also be a fever and high-pitched noisy sounds when breathing [2]. Symptoms are usually worse at night, and last for about three to five days [2]. According to the NHS, symptoms include a barking cough that may sound like a seal, a hoarse voice, difficulty breathing, and a high pitched, rasping sound when breathing in [1]. Your child will usually have cold-like symptoms to begin with, such as a temperature, runny nose and cough, and croup symptoms usually come on after a few days and are often worse at night [1].

Children between the ages of 6 months and 3 years have the highest risk of getting croup, and they may also have more severe symptoms [2]. Croup is more common in the fall and winter [2]. Most cases of viral croup are mild and can be treated at home, but rarely croup can become serious and interfere with your child's breathing [2]. Croup is a common condition that mainly affects babies' and young children's airways, and it is usually mild [4].

## What you can do at home

Croup usually gets better on its own within 48 hours [5]. If a doctor confirms your child has mild croup, there are things you can do to help them feel better [5]:

- Sit your child upright and try not to let them lie down [5].
- Comfort them if they're distressed and try to keep them calm, because crying can make the symptoms worse [5].
- Give them plenty of fluids [5].
- Check on them regularly, including at night [5].
- Give them paracetamol or ibuprofen to help ease a high temperature or any discomfort [5].

Do not put your child in a steamy room or get them to inhale steam [6]. Water can help ease a cough, whether you drink it or add it to the air with a steamy shower or vaporizer [3]. Children under four should not have cough medicine; for children over four, use caution and read labels carefully [3].

## When to see a doctor or go to the emergency department

Ask for an urgent appointment or contact your doctor if you think your child may have croup, because the doctor may give your child medicine to shorten the illness, or if your child is getting worse or not getting better after seeing a doctor [1]. Occasionally, your child may be referred to hospital if they're more seriously ill or they're under the age of 3 months [1]. If you are worried about your child's breathing, call your health care provider right away [2].

Call your local emergency number or go to the emergency department if your child:

- is struggling to breathe (you may notice grunting noises, see their tummy sucking under their ribcage, or their breathing may sound different) [6]
- has blue, grey, pale or blotchy skin, lips or tongue – on brown or black skin, this may be easier to see on the palms of the hands or soles of the feet [6]
- is sleepier than normal or difficult to wake [6]
- is unusually quiet and still [6]
- is limp, floppy or not responding like they normally do (their head may fall to the side, backwards or forwards, or they may find it difficult to lift their head or focus on your face) [6]
- is very upset or restless, is having difficulty breathing and can't be calmed down [6]
- is unwell and drooling more than they normally do, finding it hard to swallow, or wants to sit rather than lie down [6]

Do not drive to the emergency department yourself. Ask someone to drive you or call your local emergency number and ask for an ambulance, and bring any medicines you take with you [6].

## Common questions

**Is croup the same as whooping cough?**
No. Whooping cough, also known as pertussis, is a very contagious respiratory illness that may begin like a common cold, but unlike a cold, the coughing can last for weeks or months [7]. It can cause coughing fits, and in serious cases the coughing can become violent and rapid, so that you may cough so hard that you vomit [8]. The name comes from the whooping noise you might make when you try to breathe in after coughing [8]. Whooping cough is especially serious in babies who did not yet get the vaccine, and about half of babies under age one who get it need care in the hospital [8]. Babies under 6 months old with whooping cough have an increased chance of problems such as dehydration, breathing difficulties, pneumonia and seizures [9]. The early symptoms in babies can be different: they may only cough a little bit, or not cough at all, and they may have a pause in breathing or start to turn blue — if this happens, get medical care for your baby right away [10].

**How long does the cough last?**
Croup symptoms usually last for about three to five days [2]. An acute cough usually lasts no more than 2 to 3 weeks, while a chronic cough lasts longer than 2 to 3 weeks [3].
