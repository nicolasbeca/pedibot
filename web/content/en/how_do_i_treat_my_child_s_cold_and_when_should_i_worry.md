## What it is

The common cold is a mild infection of the upper respiratory tract, which includes the nose and throat [9]. It is probably the most common illness: adults have an average of 2 to 3 colds a year, and children have even more [9]. Colds are more common in winter and spring, but you can get them at any time [9].

More than 200 different viruses can cause a cold, and rhinoviruses are the most common type [2]. These viruses are very contagious. They spread from person to person through the air and through close personal contact [2]. You can also get infected when you touch something that has the virus on it and then touch your eyes, mouth or nose — for example, after shaking hands with someone who has a cold or touching a doorknob with germs on it [2].

The symptoms usually start a few days after infection and can include sneezing, a stuffy or runny nose, sore throat, coughing and headache [2]. Some symptoms can last 10 to 14 days [2]. Most people with cold symptoms will not need specific treatment [1]. There is no cure for the common cold, but there are treatments that can make your child feel better while the cold goes away on its own [6].

## What you can do at home

- Give your child lots of rest [6].
- Make sure they drink plenty of fluids [6].
- Use a clean humidifier or cool mist vaporizer [6].
- Gargling with warm salt water can help an older child [6].
- Saline nose drops or sprays can help a blocked nose [6].
- You can use over-the-counter pain or cold and cough medicines, but be careful with certain medicines [6].
- Children and teens should not take aspirin [6].
- Some cold and cough medicines contain ingredients that are not recommended for children. Talk with your child's health care provider before giving your child any cold and cough medicines [6].
- Some cold and cough medicines already contain pain relievers. If you also give a separate pain reliever, your child could get a dangerous amount. Read the labels and follow the instructions carefully, and ask your provider or a pharmacist if you have questions [6].
- Antibiotics will not help with a cold. Antibiotics help with bacterial infections, not with viral infections such as colds [6].

To reduce the risk of getting or spreading a cold, wash hands often with soap and water for at least 20 seconds, avoid touching the face with unwashed hands, avoid close contact such as kissing or sharing cups and utensils with someone who is sick, clean frequently touched surfaces, cover coughs and sneezes with a tissue and then throw it away and wash hands, and stay home when sick [5]. There is no vaccine to protect against the common cold [5].

## When to see a doctor or go to the emergency department

Most people who have a cold will feel better after a week or two [6]. However, some people who get a cold may develop other illnesses, such as bronchitis or pneumonia. This is more common in people with weakened immune systems, asthma or other respiratory conditions [6]. Rarely, a viral infection can lead to a bacterial infection, like pneumonia, which requires antibiotics [1].

Contact your doctor if you or your child have symptoms that concern you, such as:

- Trouble breathing or fast breathing [6]
- Dehydration [6]
- Fever that lasts longer than 4 days [6]
- Symptoms that last more than 10 days without improvement [6]
- Symptoms, such as fever or cough, that improve but then return or worsen [6]
- Worsening of chronic medical conditions [6]

Get medical help if you are worried your child is seriously ill [8]. Ask for urgent advice if:

- Your child is unwell and it is getting worse [8]
- Your child is feeding or eating much less than normal [8]
- Your child has had a dry nappy for 12 hours or more, or shows other signs of dehydration [8]
- Your baby feels hotter than usual when you touch their back or chest, feels sweaty or is shivering [8]
- Your baby is under 3 months old and has a temperature of 38C, or is older than 3 months and has a temperature of 39C or higher [8]
- Your child is very tired or irritable [8]
- You get cold symptoms and you have a long-term medical condition, such as a heart or lung condition, or a weakened immune system [8]
- You have difficulty breathing — you may be shorter of breath than usual [8]

## Common questions

**How long will it last?** Symptoms usually start a few days after infection, and some can last 10 to 14 days [2]. Most people feel better after a week or two [6].

**Is it a cold or flu?** Cold and flu symptoms are similar, but flu tends to be more severe [10]. Flu appears quickly within a few hours, affects more than just your nose and throat, and makes you feel exhausted and too unwell to carry on as normal. A cold appears gradually over 1 to 2 days, affects mainly your nose and throat, and makes you feel unwell but still well enough to do your normal activities [10].

**Could it be something else?** Cold-like symptoms can also be the start of croup, whooping cough or RSV [3][4][8]. Croup causes a barking cough that may sound like a seal, a hoarse voice and difficulty breathing, and is often worse at night [3]. Whooping cough starts like a cold, but after about a week brings coughing bouts that last for a few minutes, often worse at night, and may make a "whoop" sound [4]. Babies with whooping cough may only cough a little or not at all, and may have pauses in breathing or turn blue — get medical care right away if this happens [7]. RSV usually causes only cold-like symptoms, but if it leads to a more serious infection it may cause a cough that gets worse, shortness of breath, faster breathing or long gaps between breaths, difficulty feeding in babies, or noisy breathing [8].
