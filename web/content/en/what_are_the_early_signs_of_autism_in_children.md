## What it is

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a neurological and developmental disorder that begins early in childhood and lasts throughout a person's life [1]. It affects how a person acts and interacts with others, communicates, and learns [1]. It is called a "spectrum" disorder because people with ASD can have a range of symptoms [1]. Signs of autism might be noticed at a very young age, or not until later in life [2]. Every autistic person is different, and many autistic people will not show all the signs, or the signs may not always be obvious [2]. Autism may also look different in someone who has other conditions, such as ADHD, an eating disorder or a learning disability [2]. Some people learn to adapt so that signs are less noticeable; this is called masking, and it can make everyday activities feel exhausting and stressful [2].

## What you can do at home

- You know your child best. If your child is not meeting one or more milestones, has lost skills they once had, or you have other concerns, act early [9].
- Talk with your child's doctor, share your concerns, and ask about developmental screening [9].
- If you or the doctor are still concerned, ask for a referral to a specialist who can evaluate your child more [9].
- Ask about early intervention services where you live, to find out if your child can get help [9].
- If your child is at school or nursery, you could also speak to the person responsible for special educational needs there [5].
- Starting support early can lead to better results [1]. Timely access to early evidence-based psychosocial interventions can improve an autistic child's ability to communicate and interact socially [8][10].

Signs to look out for in young children include starting to speak later than other children, taking longer to understand what others are saying, not responding to their name, not speaking much or using single words, speaking in a flat or different tone of voice, and repeating words and phrases [4]. They may focus intensely on things that interest them, use eye contact less often, and use fewer facial expressions and gestures [4]. In behaviour and play, autistic children may get too physically close to people, not want to be cuddled, find it hard to join in playing with other children, not take part in pretend play, enjoy repetitive actions, or do repetitive movements such as rocking, spinning or flapping their hands [7]. They may be worried or upset by changes in routine or new situations [7]. Sensory differences are also common: reacting very strongly, or not at all, to lights, sounds, smells, tastes or textures, not liking brightly lit or noisy rooms, or only eating certain foods or foods of certain textures [6]. Older children may have few close friends, take things literally, need to follow rules, or find it difficult to adjust to changes in routine [5]. It can be harder to see some signs in girls because of masking [3].

## When to see a doctor or go to the emergency department

Speak to your doctor if you think your child might be autistic [5]. If the doctor thinks your child may be autistic, they can refer them for an autism assessment and let you know if there is support available near you [5]. Do not wait: if your child is not meeting one or more milestones, has lost skills, or you have other concerns, act early [9]. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that children be screened for general development using standardized, validated tools at 9, 18, and 30 months and for autism at 18 and 24 months, or whenever a parent or provider has a concern [9]. These ages come from the United States, and schedules change from country to country. If your child is in immediate danger or seriously unwell, contact your local emergency number or go to the emergency department.

## Common questions

**Is autism caused by vaccines?** No. Extensive research over many years has shown that the measles, mumps and rubella vaccine does not cause autism [8]. The study that was interpreted as showing such a link was later proven wrong and fraudulent, the journal withdrew it, and the author lost his medical licence [8]. Evidence also shows that other childhood vaccines do not increase the risk of autism [8].

**What causes autism?** The causes are not known [1]. Scientific evidence suggests there are probably many factors that make a child more likely to have autism, including environmental and genetic factors [8]. Factors that appear more often in children with autism or their parents include advanced parental age, maternal diabetes during pregnancy, exposure to air pollutants or certain heavy metals before birth, prematurity, severe birth complications and low birth weight [8]. More research is needed to understand how these factors interact with genetic variations [8].

**Is there a treatment?** There is currently no one standard treatment for autism [1]. There are many ways to increase your child's ability to grow and learn new skills, and starting early can lead to better results [1]. Treatments include behaviour and communication therapies, skills training, and medicines to control symptoms [1]. A broad range of interventions across the life span can improve development, health, well-being and quality of life [8][10].
