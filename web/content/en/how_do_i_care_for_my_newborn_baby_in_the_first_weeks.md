## What it is

Going home with a new baby is exciting, but it can also feel scary [3]. Newborns have many needs, such as frequent feeding and nappy changes [3]. Babies can have health problems that are different from those of older children and adults, such as nappy rash and cradle cap [3]. Your baby will go through many changes during the first year of life, and you may feel uneasy at first [3]. Ask your health care provider for help if you need it [3].

Newborn care in the first weeks includes keeping the baby warm, feeding, cord care, eye care and hygiene [2]. It also includes weighing the baby, checking the baby's temperature, and finding and treating illness early [2]. Babies who are born early (preterm) or with a low birth weight need extra visits and extra support [2].

## What you can do at home

- Feed your baby often. Support exclusive breastfeeding, and ask for help with positioning and attachment if feeding is difficult [2].
- Keep your baby warm. Preventing heat loss is part of immediate newborn care [9].
- Keep the cord clean and dry [6].
- Wash your hands. Promoting hand washing is part of home newborn care [2].
- Avoid early bathing and bottle feeding [2].
- Check the nappies. Babies do 2 or more poos a day in the first week of life, going down to about 2 a day by 1 year [5]. Breastfed babies may poo at each feed in the early weeks, then after about 6 weeks may not poo for several days [5]. Formula-fed babies may poo up to 5 times a day when newborn, and after a few months this can go down to once a day [5].
- Do not worry if your baby strains or cries when doing a poo. Your baby is not constipated as long as the poos are soft, even if there has been no poo for a few days [5].
- Expect the first poo, called meconium, to be sticky and greenish-black, usually in the first 48 hours [7]. After a few days it changes to a yellow or mustard colour [7]. Breastfed babies' poo is runny and does not smell; formula-fed babies' poo is firmer, darker brown and more smelly [7].
- If you have a girl, you may see a white discharge on her nappy for a few days after birth, and sometimes slight bleeding like a mini period. This is nothing to worry about [7].
- Choose nappies that suit your baby. Disposable and cloth nappies come in many shapes and sizes, and you will work out what suits your baby best as they grow [8].

## When to see a doctor or go to the emergency department

Newborn illness can be hard to spot, and early identification matters [2]. Get medical help if your baby:

- is not feeding well, or is feeding much less than usual [2]
- is not gaining weight, or is losing weight [2]
- feels cold, or is not staying warm [2]
- has a change in the cord area, or the cord does not stay clean and dry [6]
- has signs of infection, such as sepsis or pneumonia [2][9]
- has jaundice (yellow skin or eyes) that worries you [4]

Jaundice is usually caused by the baby's liver not working fully straight after birth, which lets a waste product called bilirubin build up [4]. In most babies, jaundice is not a sign of another health condition, and levels return to normal after a few weeks [4]. Jaundice can also be caused by infections such as rubella, by the baby having a different blood type from the mother, or by problems with how the liver works [4].

If you are worried, contact your doctor or your local emergency number, or go to the emergency department.

## Common questions

**How often should my baby poo?**
Two or more poos a day in the first week, then about 2 a day by 1 year [5]. Breastfed and formula-fed babies differ, and both patterns can be normal [5].

**My baby has not pooed for days. Is that constipation?**
Not if the poos are soft. It is normal for babies to strain or cry when doing a poo [5].

**What should the first poo look like?**
Sticky and greenish-black. This is meconium, and it usually comes in the first 48 hours [7].

**Is a white discharge in my baby girl's nappy normal?**
Yes. It is caused by hormones from the mother and soon disappears [7].

**Who can help me at home?**
In some health systems, a trained health worker visits the family at home during the first six weeks to check the baby, support breastfeeding and look for illness [2]. Ask your health care provider what is available where you live [3].
