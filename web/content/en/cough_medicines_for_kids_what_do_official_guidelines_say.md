## What the guidelines say

| Organisation | What it says | Source |
|---|---|---|
| CDC | OTC cough/cold medicines are not recommended for children younger than 6 years and can cause serious, sometimes life-threatening side effects. For fever, acetaminophen or ibuprofen may be used, but ask a healthcare provider about the correct dose. Honey can relieve cough for children at least 1 year old; never give honey to babies under 1 year. | [1] |
| MedlinePlus | Children under 4 should not have cough medicine. For children over 4, use caution and read labels carefully. Talk to your child's healthcare provider before giving any cold or cough medicine. Children and teens should not take aspirin. | [2][4] |
| NHS | Do not give aspirin to children under 16 years. For whooping cough, you can take paracetamol or ibuprofen if uncomfortable—check the leaflet to confirm it is suitable for your child. The NHS does not recommend OTC cough medicines for young children in its cold guidance. | [9] |

## Where they agree

All three organisations advise against giving over-the-counter cough and cold medicines to young children, though the age cut-offs differ slightly (CDC says under 6, MedlinePlus says under 4). All agree that aspirin should not be given to children or teens. They also agree that supportive care—rest, fluids, and honey (for children over 1 year)—is the safer approach. None of the sources recommend a specific OTC cough medicine for children.

## Where they differ (and why it may not matter)

The main difference is the age threshold: CDC says avoid OTC cough/cold medicines under 6 years, while MedlinePlus says children under 4 should not have cough medicine. This difference likely reflects evolving evidence rather than a true disagreement—both are saying that very young children should not receive these products. The NHS does not give a specific age for OTC cough medicines in the sources provided, but it does advise against aspirin under 16 and recommends paracetamol or ibuprofen for discomfort. The practical takeaway is the same: for young children, skip the cough syrup and use non-drug measures first.

## When to see a doctor or go to the emergency department

Seek urgent medical advice if your child has a barking cough, hoarse voice, or difficulty breathing—these can be signs of croup [3]. Go to the emergency department immediately if your child's lips, tongue, face, or skin turn blue or grey, if they are struggling to breathe, have chest pain worse when breathing or coughing, or have seizures [5]. Also seek urgent help if your child is under 6 months old with whooping cough symptoms, or if a cough lasts more than 10 days without improvement, fever lasts over 4 days, or symptoms improve then return or worsen [2][5]. If your child has asthma and quick-relief medicines do not work during an attack, get medical help right away [8].
