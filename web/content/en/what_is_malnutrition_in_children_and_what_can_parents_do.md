## What it is

Malnutrition refers to deficiencies, excesses or imbalances in a person's intake of energy and/or nutrients [3]. The term covers three broad groups of conditions [3]. The first is undernutrition, which includes wasting (low weight-for-height), stunting (low height-for-age) and underweight (low weight-for-age) [3]. The second is micronutrient-related malnutrition, which includes a lack of important vitamins and minerals, or too much of them [3]. The third is overweight, obesity and diet-related noncommunicable diseases such as heart disease, stroke, diabetes and some cancers [3].

Undernutrition has four broad sub-forms: wasting, stunting, underweight, and deficiencies in vitamins and minerals [9]. Wasting is low weight-for-height, and it usually shows recent and severe weight loss because a person has not had enough food and/or has had an infectious disease such as diarrhoea [9]. Stunting is low height-for-age, and it results from chronic or recurrent undernutrition, usually linked to poor socioeconomic conditions, poor maternal health and nutrition, frequent illness, and/or inappropriate infant and young child feeding and care in early life [9]. Stunting holds children back from reaching their physical and cognitive potential [9]. A child who is underweight may be stunted, wasted or both [9].

Micronutrients enable the body to produce enzymes, hormones and other substances that are essential for proper growth and development [4]. Iodine, vitamin A and iron are the most important in global public health terms, and their deficiency is a major threat to health and development, particularly for children and pregnant women in low-income countries [4].

Women, infants, children and adolescents are at particular risk of malnutrition [1]. Optimising nutrition early in life, including the 1000 days from conception to a child's second birthday, ensures the best possible start in life with long-term benefits [1]. Poverty amplifies the risk of, and risks from, malnutrition [1]. According to the WHO, nearly half of deaths among children under 5 years of age are linked to undernutrition, mostly in low- and middle-income countries [7].

## What you can do at home

- Feed your child a variety of foods each day so the diet is not based on one or two items [3].
- Pay attention to the first 1000 days, from conception to the second birthday, as this is the period when nutrition matters most for long-term development [1].
- Include foods that provide iodine, vitamin A and iron, since a lack of these is a major threat to children's health and development [4].
- If your child has diarrhoea or another illness that causes weight loss, treat the illness and watch their weight, because illness is a common cause of wasting [9].
- If you are pregnant or breastfeeding, your own nutrition matters for your child's growth [9].
- If you are worried about your child's weight or height, ask your doctor to measure and plot them rather than judging by eye [9].

## When to see a doctor or go to the emergency department

- Your child looks very thin for their height, or has lost weight recently [9].
- Your child is much shorter than other children of the same age [9].
- Your child is underweight, or you are not sure whether they are growing well [9].
- Your child has diarrhoea or another infection and is losing weight [9].
- Your child is not eating or drinking normally, or is becoming weak or very tired [9].
- Your child is moderately or severely wasted — this increases the risk of death, but treatment is possible [9].
- You are pregnant or breastfeeding and cannot get enough varied food yourself [1][9].

If you are worried, contact your doctor. In an emergency, call your local emergency number or go to the nearest emergency department.

## Common questions

**Is malnutrition only about not eating enough?**
No. It also includes eating too much or the wrong balance of nutrients, and it covers overweight, obesity and diet-related diseases such as heart disease, stroke, diabetes and some cancers [3][8].

**Can malnutrition affect a child who eats every day?**
Yes. A child can eat every day and still lack important vitamins and minerals, or eat mainly poor-quality food [3][4].

**Does malnutrition only happen in poor countries?**
No. Every country in the world is affected by one or more forms of malnutrition [1].

**Can a wasted child get better?**
Yes. A young child who is moderately or severely wasted has an increased risk of death, but treatment is possible [9].

**How many children are affected?**
Globally in 2022, 149 million children under 5 were estimated to be stunted, 45 million were estimated to be wasted, and 37 million were overweight or living with obesity [7].
