## What it is

Heat exhaustion happens when your child gets too hot and loses too much water and salt through sweating [6]. It can happen after several days of being in high temperatures without enough fluids [6]. Heat exhaustion is not usually an emergency if you can cool your child down within 30 minutes [1]. But if it is not treated, it can turn into heatstroke [6].

Heatstroke is a medical emergency [3]. It happens when the body's temperature rises to dangerous levels, sometimes above 106°F (41°C) in minutes [6]. Heatstroke needs to be treated as an emergency [1].

Symptoms of heat exhaustion include [1]:
- Tiredness
- Dizziness
- Headache
- Feeling sick (nausea) or being sick (vomiting)
- Excessive sweating and skin becoming pale and clammy (this can be harder to see on brown or black skin)
- Cramps in the arms, legs, and stomach
- A high temperature
- Being very thirsty
- Feeling irritable

Symptoms of heatstroke include [3]:
- A very high temperature
- Hot skin without sweating
- Fast breathing and a fast heartbeat
- Confusion and restlessness
- Seizure (fit)
- Loss of consciousness

## What you can do at home

If your child has heat exhaustion, follow these 4 steps [5]:
1. Move them to a cool place.
2. Remove all unnecessary clothing like a jacket or socks.
3. Give them plenty of water to drink. You can also give an isotonic sports drink or oral rehydration powder mixed with water to replace salt lost through sweating.
4. Cool their skin – spray or sponge them with cool water and fan them. Cold packs wrapped in a cloth and put under the armpits or on the neck are good too.

Stay with them until they are better [5]. They should start to cool down and feel better within 30 minutes [5].

To prevent heat exhaustion or heatstroke [2]:
- Give your child more cold drinks, especially if they are active or exercising
- Dress them in light-coloured, loose clothing
- Avoid the sun between 11am and 3pm
- If you are inside on a very hot day, close curtains and close windows if it is hotter outside than in your home
- Turn off electrical equipment and lights that get hot

Check on children, older people, and people with long-term health conditions, as they are more at risk of heat exhaustion or heatstroke [2]. Young children are most at risk [6].

## When to see a doctor or go to the emergency department

Get help from NHS 111 now if you or your child have symptoms of heat exhaustion that you are struggling to treat or you need advice about [5].

Call 999 now if your child has signs of heatstroke, including [5]:
- Still being unwell after 30 minutes of resting in a cool place, being cooled, and drinking fluids
- A very high temperature
- Hot skin that is not sweating and might look red (this can be harder to see on brown or black skin)
- A fast heartbeat
- Fast breathing or shortness of breath
- Confusion and lack of coordination
- A seizure or fit
- Loss of consciousness

Do not drive yourself to A&E [5]. The person you speak to at 999 will give you advice about what to do [5].

While waiting for help with heatstroke [4]:
- Wrap your child in a cool, wet sheet, or fan them or sponge them down with cold water
- If they lose consciousness, put them in the recovery position and tell the ambulance service
- Check their breathing and pulse and carry out CPR if they show no sign of life

## Common questions

**What is the difference between heat exhaustion and heatstroke?**
Heat exhaustion is less serious and can be treated at home if you cool down within 30 minutes [1]. Heatstroke is a medical emergency that needs immediate help [3]. Heat exhaustion can turn into heatstroke if it is not treated [6].

**Can I give my child a sports drink?**
Yes. You can give an isotonic sports drink or oral rehydration powder mixed with water to help replace salt lost through sweating [5].

**Who is most at risk of heat illness?**
Older adults, young children, and those who are sick or overweight are most at risk [6]. Taking certain medicines or drinking alcohol can also raise the risk [6].
