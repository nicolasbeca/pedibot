## What it is

Whooping cough, also called pertussis, is a very contagious infection of the lungs and breathing tubes [3][5]. It is caused by bacteria called Bordetella pertussis [5][7]. The bacteria damage the tiny hair-like extensions in the airways and cause swelling [5]. The disease spreads from person to person through coughing, sneezing, or breathing very close to someone [7]. It can also spread by touching an infected surface and then touching your nose or mouth [7].

The first signs look like a cold, such as a runny nose and sore throat [3]. A high temperature is uncommon [3]. Symptoms usually start 5 to 10 days after exposure, but can appear up to 3 weeks later [6]. After about a week, coughing bouts begin [3]. These fits can last for a few minutes and are often worse at night [3]. Your child may make a "whoop" sound when gasping for breath between coughs [3]. Young babies under 3 months old may not make this sound [3]. Your child may bring up thick mucus, vomit, or become very red in the face [3][6]. The cough can last for several weeks or months [3][6].

Whooping cough is especially serious in babies [1]. About half of babies under one year old who get it need hospital care [1]. Babies under 6 months have a higher chance of problems like dehydration, breathing difficulties, pneumonia, and seizures [9]. Older children may have sore ribs, hernias, or pee leaking when they cough [9].

## What you can do at home

- Keep your child away from others, especially babies and pregnant women, because whooping cough spreads easily [3][4].
- Wash hands often with soap and water, or use an alcohol-based hand rub [4].
- Clean and disinfect surfaces you touch often, including toys [4].
- Cover coughs and sneezes with a tissue or your upper sleeve, not your hands [4].
- Stay home when sick [4].
- Make sure your child rests and drinks fluids to avoid dehydration [9].
- Do not give any medication doses without checking the dose calculator first.

Antibiotics can help if given early [8]. They may make the infection less serious and shorten the time your child is contagious [7][8]. Sometimes doctors give antibiotics to family members who were exposed, to prevent the illness [4]. Talk to your doctor about whether antibiotics are right for your child.

## When to see a doctor or go to the emergency department

Get medical care right away if your baby has a pause in breathing or starts to turn blue [6]. Young infants may also turn blue or grey after a coughing bout [3]. Seek urgent help if your child has difficulty breathing after coughing [3]. Watch for signs of dehydration, which is a risk for babies under 6 months [9]. If your child has a seizure or fit, get emergency care [9]. Go to the emergency department if your child seems very unwell or you are worried about their breathing.

## Common questions

**Can my child get whooping cough if vaccinated?** Vaccines are the best way to prevent whooping cough [4]. The vaccine is given as part of routine childhood vaccinations [2]. But no vaccine is perfect, and symptoms can vary based on whether a child has been vaccinated [5]. Vaccinated children may have milder illness [5].

**How long is my child contagious?** Your child is contagious for about 2 weeks after the cough starts [7]. Antibiotics may shorten this time [7].

**Can I protect my baby before birth?** Yes. Pregnant women should get the whooping cough vaccine, usually around 20 weeks of pregnancy [2]. This helps protect the baby for the first few weeks of life [2]. Speak to your GP or midwife if you are 20 weeks pregnant and have not been offered it [2].

**Who is most at risk?** Babies younger than 1 year old are at greatest risk for severe complications [5]. Many babies catch it from older siblings, parents, or caregivers who do not know they have it [10]. Keep babies away from anyone with a cough [4].
