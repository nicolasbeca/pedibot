## What it is

Anaphylaxis is a serious, life-threatening allergic reaction. It happens when the body overreacts to something it is allergic to [7]. Common triggers include foods like nuts, cow's milk, eggs, fish, or sesame seeds; medicines such as antibiotics or NSAIDs; insect stings from wasps or bees; anaesthetics; and latex [7]. Sometimes the cause is unknown [7].

Symptoms can affect any part of the body, often several parts at once [9]. These include feeling dizzy or lightheaded, itchy skin or a raised rash (hives), swelling of the lips, face, and eyes, coughing, wheezing, breathlessness, noisy breathing, or a hoarse voice [9]. Other signs are sneezing, an itchy or runny nose, feeling sick, vomiting, tummy pain, or diarrhoea [9]. Symptoms may appear right after eating the trigger food or days later [9]. Note that oral allergy syndrome — an itchy mouth and throat after raw fruit, vegetables, or nuts — is usually not serious and can be helped by cooking the food [8].

## What you can do at home

If you think your child is having anaphylaxis, act fast [5]. Use an adrenaline auto-injector (such as an EpiPen) immediately if one is available — instructions are printed on the side of the device [5]. Call 999 for an ambulance and say you suspect anaphylaxis [5]. Lay your child down and raise their legs; if they are struggling to breathe, raise their shoulders or sit them up slowly [5]. If an insect stung them, try to remove the sting if it is still in the skin [5]. If symptoms have not improved after 5 minutes, use a second adrenaline auto-injector [5]. Do not let your child stand or walk, even if they feel better [5].

Call 999 after using the injector or nasal spray, even if your child seems to improve [3]. Anaphylaxis requires hospital treatment immediately [2]. In hospital, treatments may include adrenaline by injection or drip, oxygen, and fluids through a drip [2]. Your child will usually stay in hospital for 2 to 12 hours, sometimes longer [2]. Before leaving, you should receive two adrenaline auto-injectors or a prescription for them [2]. You will be shown how and when to use them each time they are prescribed [2]. An allergy specialist may see you for tests and advice [2].

To prevent future reactions, avoid the food, medicine, or trigger entirely [1]. Check food labels carefully and tell restaurant and café staff about the allergy [1][4]. Carry two adrenaline auto-injectors or nasal sprays at all times if prescribed [1][4]. Check expiry dates regularly and replace them before they expire [1]. Practise with a trainer injector (no needle or medicine) and teach friends, family, and school staff how and when to use the injector [1]. Wear medical alert jewellery describing the allergy [1]. Do not store auto-injectors somewhere too hot or cold, like a fridge or in direct sun [1]. Tell airlines and cabin staff about the allergy before flying [4]. Do not cut foods from your child's diet without speaking to a GP [4]. Children with peanut allergy may have immunotherapy to reduce sensitivity, but they should still avoid peanuts [6].

## When to see a doctor or go to the emergency department

Go to the emergency department or call 999 if your child shows signs of a severe allergic reaction [5]. Use the adrenaline auto-injector first if you have one, then call for an ambulance [5]. Even if your child feels better after the injector, you must still call 999 [3]. Do not wait to see if symptoms pass — anaphylaxis is an emergency [2][5].

## Common questions

**How do I use an adrenaline auto-injector?** Different types are given differently [5]. Instructions are included on the side of the injector [5]. Use a trainer injector to practise regularly [1]. If you forget how to use it, instructions come with the device [3].

**Can my child outgrow a food allergy?** The sources do not address this. An allergy specialist can provide tests and advice after a reaction [2]. Tests may include skin-prick tests, blood tests, or an elimination diet [10]. You may also keep a food and symptoms diary [10].

**What is the difference between a food allergy and intolerance?** A food allergy can be serious and symptoms may appear straight away or days later [9]. A food intolerance causes symptoms like bloating and tummy pain, usually a few hours after eating [9].
